HEAT ILLNESS PREVENTION PLAN

ABR Quality Resources Inc dba Hamilton Exteriors 21634 Redwood Rd Unit F, Castro Valley, CA 94546 · CSLB 1078806 Cal/OSHA 8 CCR §3395 — Heat Illness Prevention in Outdoor Places of Employment
Effective date
Responsible person Alex Li, President —
Applies to All outdoor work. Roofing is a high-heat industry under §3395(e).

§3395 requires this plan to be in writing, in English and in the language understood by the majority of the employees, and to be available at the worksite to employees and to the Division on request. The Spanish version is heat-illness-prevention-plan-es.md. Both go in every company vehicle.

A roof deck runs far hotter than the air temperature. Treat the forecast as a floor, not a measurement.

1. Water

  • Hamilton provides potable drinking water, free, at the jobsite, located as close as practicable to where the crew is working.
  • At least one quart per employee per hour — two gallons per employee for an 8-hour shift — is at the site at the start of the shift, or Hamilton has effective procedures for replenishment during the shift.
  • Water is fresh, pure, suitably cool, and free of charge. Coolers are iced and refilled as needed. Nobody pays for water and nobody is expected to bring his own.
  • The foreman encourages the crew to drink about one cup every 15 minutes and says so out loud, not once at the start of the day.

2. Shade

  • Shade is present and open to the crew whenever the temperature exceeds 80°F.
  • When it is at or below 80°F, shade is provided promptly on request by any employee.
  • There is enough shade for all employees on a recovery or rest period and on a meal period at once, seated normally, without touching each other.
  • Shade is as close as practicable to the work area and does not itself expose anyone to a hazard. A closed vehicle is shade only if it is air conditioned and running.
  • Shade is either a canopy/pop-up Hamilton brings, or a permanent structure or tree that blocks direct sun so the crew can see their skin is not in the sun.

3. Cool-down rest

  • Any employee may take a cool-down rest in the shade of at least 5 minutes whenever he feels he needs it to protect himself from overheating. He does not need permission and does not need to explain.
  • No employee is ordered back to work until the signs and symptoms of heat illness are gone.
  • An employee on a cool-down rest is monitored and asked whether he is experiencing symptoms.
  • Cool-down rest time is paid. It is not a meal period and it is not the paid 10-minute rest period — it is on top of both.
  • If an employee shows or reports symptoms of heat illness during a cool-down rest, first aid or emergency response begins immediately. He is not left alone and he is not sent home alone.

4. High-heat procedures — at or above 95°F

Roofing is one of the industries §3395(e) names. When the temperature equals or exceeds 95°F, the foreman additionally:

  • Maintains effective communication by voice, observation, or electronic means so any employee can contact a supervisor. If a radio or phone is the method, it is tested and it works at that site.
  • Observes employees for signs and symptoms of heat illness, by one of: supervisor or designee observing 20 or fewer employees, a mandatory buddy system, or regular communication with an employee working alone.
  • Designates one or more employees on each site authorized to call for emergency medical services, and lets any employee call when no designated person is available.
  • Reminds employees throughout the shift to drink plenty of water.
  • Holds a pre-shift meeting before work begins to review the high-heat procedures, encourage water, and remind everyone of his right to a cool-down rest.

5. Acclimatization

Heat kills new people. Most California heat fatalities happen in the first days on the job.

  • All employees are closely observed by a supervisor or designee during a heat wave. A heat wave means any day the predicted high is at or above 80°F and at least 10°F higher than the average high of the preceding five days.
  • A new employee, or an employee newly assigned to a high heat area, is closely observed by a supervisor or designee for his first 14 days of employment.
  • New employees are eased in: shorter exposure the first days, more frequent cool-down rests, lighter assignments, and the foreman checking on them by name.
  • Employees are told that symptoms in the first days are expected to be watched for, not hidden, and that reporting them costs them nothing.

6. Emergency response procedures

  1. Ensure effective communication — every crew has a working phone. If a site has no signal, the foreman identifies before work starts where signal exists and tells the crew.
  2. Respond to symptoms. Anyone showing signs of possible heat illness is checked and is never left alone or sent home without being offered on-site first aid or emergency medical services.
  3. Call 911 immediately if an employee shows signs of heat stroke — loss of consciousness, confusion, slurred speech, seizure, hot dry skin, very high temperature. Heat stroke kills in minutes. Do not drive him yourself, do not wait to see if he improves.
  4. Cool him while waiting — into the shade, loosen or remove outer clothing, cool water or ice to the head, neck, armpits, groin, fan him. Do not give fluids to someone who is not fully conscious.
  5. Give clear directions to the site. The foreman knows the street address and nearest cross street before work starts, and sends someone to the street to flag the ambulance.
  6. If the site has no street address, the foreman records coordinates or a clear landmark description before work starts.
  7. Notify Alex Li the same day, and follow the IIPP reporting steps. A heat illness that results in inpatient hospitalization is a serious injury reportable to Cal/OSHA within 8 hours at 1-800-963-9424.

7. Symptoms every employee is trained to recognize

Heat exhaustion Heat stroke — call 911
Heavy sweating, cool clammy skin Hot skin, may be dry, may still be sweating
Headache, dizziness, weakness Confusion, slurred speech, strange behavior
Nausea, vomiting Seizure, loss of consciousness
Muscle cramps Very high body temperature
Fast weak pulse Fast strong pulse

Also trained: personal risk factors — a recent illness, dehydration, alcohol the night before, some medications, age, weight, and not being used to the heat.

8. Training

Before any employee works outdoors, and to every supervisor, Hamilton trains on:

  • The environmental and personal risk factors for heat illness
  • Hamilton's procedures for water, shade, cool-down rest, high heat, acclimatization
  • The employee's right to water, shade, and a cool-down rest, and that asking for them carries no penalty
  • The importance of drinking small amounts often, and of acclimatization
  • Types, signs, and symptoms of heat illness, and the importance of reporting them immediately, in himself or in a coworker
  • That heat illness may progress quickly to serious illness or death
  • Hamilton's emergency response procedures and how to give directions to the site

Supervisors are additionally trained on all of the above before supervising, on the steps to take when an employee shows symptoms, and on how to monitor and respond to weather reports and heat advisories.

Every training is recorded on safety-training-roster.md.

9. Weather monitoring

The foreman checks the forecast high for the jobsite city every morning before the crew leaves, and records it on the tailgate log. If the forecast is 80°F or higher, shade goes on the truck. If it is 95°F or higher, high-heat procedures are announced at the pre-shift meeting and the start time moves earlier where the schedule allows.
