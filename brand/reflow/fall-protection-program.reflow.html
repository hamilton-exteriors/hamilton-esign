Falls are the number one killer in roofing. This program is the part of Hamilton's IIPP that covers them.

1. THE TRIGGER HEIGHT IS 6 FEET

Effective July 1, 2025, Cal/OSHA lowered the fall protection trigger height for residential construction and roofing from 15 feet to 6 feet.

Every employee working at 6 feet or more above the grade or level below must be protected from falling.

  • Slopes 0:12 up to and including 7:12: the trigger is a 6 foot fall distance.
  • Slopes steeper than 7:12: fall protection is required REGARDLESS OF HEIGHT. There is no minimum on a steep roof. Six feet is not a floor you get to work under.
  • Fall height is measured as the vertical distance from the walking or working surface to the ground level below.
  • Parapet height is not included in the roof height measurement.
  • Anyone who learned the old 15-foot rule is working from a number that no longer exists. Do not use it.

Acceptable protection is a guardrail system, a personal fall arrest system, a safety net system, or another system accepted by Cal/OSHA for the work being done.

2. Before anyone goes up — the pre-work assessment

The foreman completes this before the first ladder is set, every job, and records it on the jobsite hazard assessment:

  • Roof height at the lowest eave and at the highest point
  • Roof pitch
  • Roof condition — rot, wet, moss, frost, loose tile, brittle panels, weak decking
  • Every skylight, hatch, chimney chase, vent opening, and hole
  • Edges, including edges hidden by overhanging material
  • Overhead power lines and their distance
  • Anchorage points available, or where anchors must be installed
  • Ladder placement and tie-off point
  • Ground control zone — where material and debris will fall, who is kept out
  • Weather: wind, rain, frost, and the day's heat forecast
  • Which system will be used, and whether the equipment for it is actually on the truck

If the required equipment is not on site, the crew does not go up. There is no version of this job that is worth a fall.

3. Systems Hamilton uses

3.1 Personal fall arrest system (PFAS) — the default on sloped residential roofs

A complete PFAS is anchorage + full body harness + connector (lanyard or self-retracting lifeline) + rope grab and lifeline where used. A body belt is not fall arrest and is never used for it.

  • Anchorage supports at least 5,000 lb per employee attached, or is designed by a qualified person with a safety factor of at least two. Ridge anchors are fastened into structural framing, never into sheathing alone, and the fastener pattern the manufacturer specifies is the pattern used.
  • Free fall is limited to 6 feet and the employee must not contact any lower level.
  • Deceleration distance is limited to 3.5 feet. Total fall clearance below the working level must be calculated before tie-off — lanyard + deceleration + harness stretch + worker height + 3 ft safety margin. If the clearance is not there, use a self-retracting lifeline or a different system.
  • The D-ring is between the shoulder blades, on the back. Front and side D-rings are for positioning and ladder climbing, not arrest.
  • Rope grabs stay above the worker. Lifelines are protected from cut edges.
  • Swing fall is planned out — work directly below the anchor where possible.

3.2 Guardrail systems

Top rail 42 inches ± 3 inches above the walking surface, midrail at about half that, toeboard where anyone can be below. Top rail withstands 200 lb applied outward or downward at the top edge without deflecting below 39 inches.

3.3 Safety nets

Where used, installed as close as practicable under the surface, never more than 30 feet below, drop-tested or certified, and inspected weekly and after any impact.

3.4 Covers for holes and skylights

Every skylight and every hole is covered and secured, or guarded, before work starts near it. A cover supports twice the weight of the employees, equipment, and materials that could be on it, is secured against displacement, and is marked "HOLE" or "COVER" in a color that stands out. A skylight dome is not a cover. Plywood laid loose is not a cover.

Never step or kneel on a skylight, and never carry material across one.

4. Ladders

  • Extend at least 3 feet above the landing surface, and are tied off or otherwise secured at the top.
  • Set at 4:1 — one foot out for every four feet of working length.
  • On firm level footing, or with levelers. Not on buckets, boxes, or unset dirt.
  • Inspected before every use. A damaged ladder is tagged and taken out of service the same day.
  • Three points of contact at all times. Material goes up by hoist or is handed up, never carried up by hand.
  • No metal ladders within 10 feet of an energized power line. Fiberglass only near electrical work.

5. Equipment inspection

  • Before every use, the employee inspects his own harness, lanyard, and connectors: webbing cuts, fraying, burns, chemical damage, stitching, D-ring distortion, hardware cracks, snap hook gate function, and the load-indicator on the shock pack.
  • Any component that has arrested a fall is immediately removed from service and destroyed. It is never returned to the truck, never "checked over and reused."
  • Damaged or suspect equipment is tagged DO NOT USE and given to the foreman the same day.
  • Hamilton documents a periodic inspection of all fall protection equipment and keeps the record.
  • Equipment is retired at the manufacturer's stated service life regardless of appearance.

6. Rescue

Suspension trauma can kill a person hanging in a harness in under 30 minutes. A plan to get him down exists before anyone ties off.

For every job the foreman identifies, before work starts:

  • How a fallen worker will be reached and lowered — ladder, lift, or by 911 response
  • Where the nearest access for a fire department ladder truck is
  • Who calls 911 and who goes to the street to flag them
  • That the jobsite street address and cross street are known and written down

If a worker falls and is suspended: call 911 immediately. Do not attempt a rescue that puts a second person at risk. If he is conscious and it is safe, have him push against the suspension trauma straps or "pump" his legs to keep blood moving until he is down.

7. Training

Before an employee works at height, and again whenever the site, equipment, or rules change, or whenever his behavior shows he did not retain it, Hamilton trains him — by a competent person — on:

  • The 6-foot trigger height and where it applies
  • The nature of the fall hazards on Hamilton's work
  • Correct procedures for erecting, maintaining, disassembling, and inspecting each system used
  • Use and operation of guardrails, PFAS, safety nets, covers, and other protection
  • The role of each employee in a safety monitoring system where one is used
  • The limitations on mechanical equipment on low-sloped roofs
  • Correct procedures for handling and storing equipment and materials, and erecting overhead protection
  • The employee's role in the fall protection and rescue plan
  • Retraining is required whenever changes in the workplace or in the systems used make the previous training obsolete.

Training is certified in writing — employee name, date of training, and the signature of the trainer — on safety-training-roster.md.

8. Discipline

Working on a roof without required fall protection is grounds for immediate termination. This applies to the foreman who allows it exactly as it applies to the employee who does it. There is no production number that justifies an exception and no one at Hamilton has the authority to grant one.

9. Weather stop-work

Work stops and the crew comes down for: sustained wind that makes footing or material handling unsafe, rain, ice or frost on the roof, lightning within audible thunder, or darkness without adequate lighting.
